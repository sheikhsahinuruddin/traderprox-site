How to Build a Trading Plan: Entry, Exit, and Risk Rules

A plan turns trading from a series of emotional guesses into a repeatable process. Here's exactly how to write one.

7 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

Ask a trader who's struggling why a particular trade went wrong, and you'll often hear something like "I don't know, I just felt like it would go up." That single sentence is usually the actual root cause, far more than any indicator or chart pattern — trading without a plan turns every decision into a fresh emotional judgment call, made in real time, under pressure, with money on the line. A trading plan replaces that with a set of decisions made in advance, when you're calm and thinking clearly.

What a trading plan actually is

A trading plan is a written set of rules that defines exactly what you'll trade, when you'll enter, when you'll exit — both for a profit and for a loss — and how much you'll risk on each trade. The point isn't to remove judgment from trading entirely; it's to move the judgment to a calmer moment, before you have a position open and your money on the line, rather than in the middle of a fast-moving trade when emotion is highest.

Traders without a plan tend to make decisions reactively: buying because price is moving up and they don't want to miss it, selling in a panic the moment a position dips, holding a loser far longer than intended because "it'll come back." A plan doesn't eliminate the temptation to do any of these things, but it gives you a written reference to check yourself against, which is a meaningfully different position than having no reference at all.

The core components of a trading plan

1. What you trade

Define the universe of instruments you'll actually trade — specific stocks, a sector, an index, or a defined watchlist — rather than trading anything that happens to catch your attention that day. Trading only what you understand and follow closely, rather than chasing whatever's moving that morning, removes an entire category of impulsive decisions.

2. Your setup — the conditions that define an opportunity

This is the heart of the plan: the specific, observable conditions that need to be true before you'll consider a trade at all. "Price pulls back to the 20-day EMA in an established uptrend, with RSI holding above 50" is a setup. "This stock looks good" is not. The more specific and observable your setup criteria are, the easier it is to judge afterward whether you actually followed your own plan or not.

3. Entry rules

Given that your setup criteria are met, exactly what triggers the actual entry? Waiting for a candlestick confirmation, a break above a specific level, or a bounce off a specific support zone are all valid entry triggers — the key is that the trigger is specific enough that two different people looking at the same chart would make the same entry decision.

4. Stop-loss rules

As covered in risk management, your stop-loss should be decided before you enter, based on where the setup itself is proven wrong — a break of the support level you bought near, for instance — not based on an arbitrary rupee amount you're comfortable losing.

5. Profit-taking rules

Define, in advance, how you'll take profit. Some traders use a fixed risk-to-reward target (exit at 2x the amount risked). Others scale out — taking partial profit at one level and letting the remainder run toward a further target. Others trail a stop-loss upward as a trade moves in their favor. Any of these can work; what matters is deciding which one you're using before you're in the trade, not making it up as price moves.

6. Position sizing rules

How much of your account you'll risk per trade, calculated the way described in TraderProX's risk management guide — based on your stop-loss distance and a fixed percentage of account risk, not a gut-feel number.

7. Trading hours and frequency

Define when you'll actually be trading and roughly how many trades you expect to take in a given period. Traders without this boundary often drift into overtrading — taking marginal setups just to stay busy — especially during quiet, low-opportunity days.

Writing rules you can actually follow

A plan that's too vague ("buy strong stocks, sell weak ones") gives you nothing concrete to check yourself against. A plan that's too complicated, with a dozen conditions that all need to line up simultaneously, will rarely trigger and is hard to evaluate honestly after the fact. The goal is somewhere in between: specific enough to be testable and repeatable, simple enough that you can actually recall and follow it in the moment.

A useful test: could you hand your written plan to another reasonably experienced trader, and would they make the same entry and exit decisions you would, on the same chart, without needing to ask you clarifying questions? If yes, the plan is specific enough. If they'd need significant guesswork to apply it, it needs tightening.

Backtesting your plan before trading it live

Once a plan is written down in specific enough terms, it becomes testable against historical data — which is exactly the point. Running your setup, entry, and exit rules against past price action tells you, roughly, how the plan would have performed, how often it wins, and how large your typical winners and losers are relative to each other. This step catches obvious problems (a setup that barely ever occurs, or one that occurs often but loses money on average) before you've risked a single rupee finding that out live.

Keeping a trading journal

A plan only improves if you track how closely you actually followed it and what happened when you did or didn't. A simple journal entry per trade — the setup that triggered it, entry and exit prices, whether the plan's rules were followed exactly, and a brief note on what happened — builds a record you can review periodically. Over dozens of trades, patterns emerge: maybe your plan performs well but you frequently exit winners too early out of nervousness, which is a discipline issue to fix, not a plan issue.

Revising a plan (carefully)

A trading plan isn't meant to be permanently fixed, but it also shouldn't be rewritten every time a single trade loses. Revising rules after every losing trade is really just abandoning the plan entirely and trading on emotion again, one trade at a time. A more sensible approach is reviewing performance after a meaningful sample size — say, 20 or 30 trades — and looking for a genuine, consistent pattern in the results before changing anything.

Practice tip: Write your setup, entry, stop-loss, and profit-taking rules down in one paragraph each, specific enough that someone else could follow them on a chart. Putting it together

A trading plan is what separates a repeatable process from a string of one-off emotional decisions. It won't guarantee profits — no plan does — but it gives you a fixed reference to measure your own discipline against, and a foundation you can actually test, refine, and improve over time. Most of the difference between traders who improve steadily and traders who repeat the same mistakes for years comes down to whether a real plan exists in the first place.
