Chart Patterns That Predict Reversals: Head and Shoulders, Double Tops & Bottoms

The patterns that show up right before a trend runs out of energy — and why waiting for confirmation matters more than spotting the shape.

7 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

Where candlestick patterns tell a story across one to three candles, chart patterns tell a story across dozens or even hundreds of candles — a shape that forms over days, weeks, or months and reflects a genuine, larger shift in the ongoing battle between buyers and sellers. Reversal patterns specifically are the ones that show up when a trend is running out of energy and is about to change direction. Learning to recognize them early, and to confirm them properly rather than jumping the gun, is a core technical analysis skill.

Why reversal patterns form at all

A trend doesn't end because of a single event — it ends because the underlying balance between buyers and sellers gradually shifts. In an uptrend, that shift usually looks like buyers pushing price to new highs with progressively less conviction, while sellers become progressively more willing to sell into strength. Reversal chart patterns are essentially a visual record of that gradual struggle, and different patterns capture slightly different versions of the same underlying story: a trend losing momentum before it actually turns.

Head and shoulders

The head and shoulders pattern is one of the most recognized reversal patterns, and it forms at the top of an uptrend. It consists of three peaks: a first peak (the left shoulder), a higher peak in the middle (the head), and a third peak roughly matching the height of the first (the right shoulder). Connecting the two low points between these peaks draws what's called the neckline.

The pattern tells a specific story. The left shoulder is a normal continuation of the existing uptrend. The head is a fresh, higher high — buyers are still technically in control. But the right shoulder fails to exceed the head's high, showing that buying momentum is now weaker than it was just one peak ago. The pattern is considered confirmed only when price breaks below the neckline, at which point the prior uptrend structure of higher highs and higher lows has genuinely broken down.

The inverse head and shoulders is the same pattern upside down, forming at the bottom of a downtrend: a low, a deeper low, and a third low that fails to exceed the depth of the middle low, with confirmation coming on a break above the neckline.

A commonly used (though rough) way to estimate a price target after confirmation is to measure the vertical distance from the head to the neckline, and project that same distance beyond the breakout point. It's a useful rule of thumb, not a guarantee.

Double tops and double bottoms

A double top forms when price rallies to a high, pulls back, rallies again to roughly the same high, and fails to push meaningfully higher the second time. It looks like the letter "M" on a chart. The failure to make a new high on the second attempt shows buyers running out of conviction at that level. The pattern is confirmed when price breaks below the low point between the two peaks (sometimes called the "support" of the pattern).

A double bottom is the mirror image at the bottom of a downtrend — a "W" shape, where price falls to a low, bounces, falls again to roughly the same low, and fails to push meaningfully lower the second time, confirmed by a break above the high point between the two troughs.

Double tops and bottoms are generally considered slightly less reliable than head and shoulders patterns simply because two touches at a similar level is a lower bar to clear than the three-peak structure of a head and shoulders — meaning more random price action can accidentally resemble a double top or bottom without an actual reversal following.

Triple tops and triple bottoms

These follow the same logic as double tops and bottoms, but with three touches at a similar level instead of two, and are generally read as a somewhat stronger signal for exactly the reason mentioned above — three failed attempts at the same level is harder to attribute to coincidence than two.

Why volume confirmation matters

Volume plays a supporting role in validating any reversal pattern. In a head and shoulders pattern, volume ideally declines from the left shoulder to the head to the right shoulder, showing progressively weaker conviction behind each successive push — and then picks up sharply on the neckline break, showing real conviction behind the reversal itself. A neckline break on unusually low volume is a weaker, less trustworthy signal than the same break accompanied by a clear volume spike.

Waiting for confirmation instead of anticipating it

The single most common mistake with reversal patterns is entering a trade based on the shape alone, before the pattern actually confirms. A head and shoulders pattern isn't "complete" until the neckline breaks — up until that point, what looks like a right shoulder forming could just as easily turn into a fresh new high, especially in a genuinely strong uptrend. Traders who short the moment the right shoulder starts to look like it's forming, without waiting for the neckline break, are trading a prediction, not a confirmed pattern.

This costs a small amount in terms of entry price — you're entering after the move has already started, rather than at the exact peak — but it dramatically reduces the number of failed patterns you trade, because you're only acting once the pattern has actually done what it needed to do to be valid.

Reading these patterns in the right context

Like every other tool covered so far, reversal patterns mean more or less depending on where they show up. A head and shoulders pattern that forms after a long, extended uptrend — one that's already stretched well above its major moving averages — carries more weight than the same shape forming after only a brief, modest rally. The pattern needs a genuine trend to reverse in the first place; looking for reversal patterns in a market that was never clearly trending tends to produce a lot of false positives.

It's also worth checking whether the pattern lines up with a known resistance level (for tops) or support level (for bottoms). A double top forming right at a major resistance zone that has rejected price multiple times before is a considerably stronger case than a double top forming at a level with no prior significance.

Common mistakes

  • Trading the pattern before confirmation. Covered above, and worth repeating — it's the most frequent and most costly error.
  • Seeing patterns everywhere. Once you learn these shapes, it's tempting to spot them constantly, including in charts that don't actually contain a clear enough version of the pattern. Genuine head and shoulders and double top/bottom patterns are less common than beginners tend to assume.
  • Ignoring the preceding trend. A reversal pattern needs a trend to reverse. The same shape forming in a flat, directionless market doesn't carry the same meaning.
  • Skipping volume confirmation. A pattern that "completes" on weak volume deserves more skepticism than one confirmed with a genuine surge in participation.
Practice tip: Scroll through a year of daily charts on a few stocks you know and try to mark every head and shoulders or double top/bottom pattern you can find, then check whether each one actually confirmed with a neckline or support/resistance break before the reversal followed through. Putting it together

Reversal chart patterns are a slower, larger-scale version of the same story candlesticks tell on a single-candle level: momentum shifting from one side to the other. Head and shoulders, double tops and bottoms, and their variations all capture a trend running out of conviction before it actually turns — but they only earn their name once confirmed with a genuine break of the pattern's key level, ideally backed by volume. Patience for that confirmation is what separates a reliable reversal trade from a guess.
