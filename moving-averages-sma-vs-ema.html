Understanding Moving Averages: SMA vs EMA and How to Use Them

Smoothing out the noise to see the trend that's actually there — and knowing which type of average to reach for.

6 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

Of all the indicators on a trading platform, the moving average is usually the first one traders learn — and for good reason. It takes something noisy and hard to read, a raw price chart jumping around every few seconds, and smooths it into a single line that shows you the underlying trend. Simple as that sounds, the details of how a moving average is calculated change how it behaves, and knowing the difference between the two most common types will change how you use them.

What a moving average actually does

A moving average takes the average closing price over a fixed number of recent periods and plots it as a single line. A 20-day simple moving average, for instance, is the average of the last 20 daily closes, recalculated fresh every day as a new day rolls in and the oldest day rolls out. As price moves, the line moves with it — but more slowly and smoothly, because it's averaging out the short-term noise.

The core idea behind using one at all is simple: raw price is noisy, and it's genuinely difficult to tell "is this a real trend or just random wiggling" by eye alone. A moving average filters out a lot of that noise and gives you a cleaner read on the underlying direction.

Simple Moving Average (SMA)

The Simple Moving Average gives every period in its lookback window equal weight. A 50-day SMA treats a close from 49 days ago exactly the same as yesterday's close — both count equally toward the average. This makes the SMA smooth and stable, but also means it reacts relatively slowly to recent changes in price, since one new data point is diluted across the full lookback period.

That slowness is not necessarily a flaw. For identifying a long-term trend, or for a level that price should respect on pullbacks (many traders watch the 50-day and 200-day SMA specifically for this), the SMA's smoothness and stability is exactly what you want. You're not trying to catch every short-term wiggle — you're trying to see through it.

Exponential Moving Average (EMA)

The Exponential Moving Average solves the "slow to react" property of the SMA by weighting recent prices more heavily than older ones. The math behind it applies a weighting factor that decreases exponentially as data gets older, which means the most recent close has a noticeably larger influence on the EMA's value than a close from three weeks ago.

The practical effect is that an EMA tracks price more closely and turns direction faster than an SMA of the same length. A 20-day EMA will start curving in a new direction sooner than a 20-day SMA would, because it's more sensitive to what just happened. This makes the EMA popular among shorter-term traders who care more about catching a move early than about smoothness.

SMA vs EMA: the actual trade-off

The choice between the two comes down to a genuine trade-off between responsiveness and stability, and there's no universally "better" answer — it depends on what you're using the moving average for.

  • EMA advantages: Reacts faster to new price action, which can mean earlier entries and exits when a trend is genuinely changing direction.
  • EMA disadvantage: That same sensitivity means it also reacts to short-term noise that isn't a real trend change, producing more false signals in choppy, sideways markets.
  • SMA advantages: Smoother and more stable, which filters out more noise and produces fewer false signals in choppy conditions.
  • SMA disadvantage: Slower to reflect a genuine, fresh change in trend, meaning entries and exits based on it tend to come later.

Many traders use both together rather than choosing one — a faster EMA to catch moves early, alongside a slower SMA to confirm the broader trend is actually pointing the same direction.

Common ways moving averages are used

Trend identification

The simplest use: if price is consistently above a rising moving average, the trend is generally considered up. If price is consistently below a falling moving average, the trend is generally considered down. When price is chopping back and forth across a flat moving average, that's often a sign there's no clear trend at all right now.

Dynamic support and resistance

In a strong trend, price frequently pulls back to touch a moving average — often the 20-period or 50-period — before continuing in the trend's direction. Traders watch these pullbacks as potential entry points, treating the moving average as a dynamic support level that moves along with the trend, rather than a fixed price.

Crossovers

A crossover strategy watches for a faster moving average crossing above or below a slower one. When a shorter-period average (say, 20-day) crosses above a longer-period average (say, 50-day), that's often called a "golden cross" and is read as a bullish signal. The reverse — the shorter average crossing below the longer one — is called a "death cross" and is read as bearish. These crossovers tend to lag actual price turns, since by the time the crossover happens, the underlying price move that caused it has usually already been underway for a while — but they're valued for confirming a trend change with less ambiguity than eyeballing price alone.

Choosing a period length

Shorter periods (like a 9-day or 20-day average) are more sensitive and better suited to shorter-term trading, where reacting quickly matters more than filtering noise. Longer periods (like a 50-day or 200-day average) are smoother and better suited to identifying the broader, longer-term trend that a swing or position trader cares about. There's no single correct period — it should match the timeframe you're actually trading on. A day trader working on 5-minute charts and a position trader holding for months are looking at fundamentally different questions, and their moving average settings should reflect that difference.

Common mistakes

  • Using a moving average alone as a complete strategy. Moving averages describe the past and current trend; they don't predict the future by themselves. Combining them with support and resistance, volume, or another indicator gives a more complete picture.
  • Fighting the trend. Trying to short a stock while it's trading firmly above a rising moving average, purely because it "feels expensive," ignores the very information the moving average is providing.
  • Over-optimizing the period length. Endlessly tweaking a moving average's length to fit exactly what happened on past charts (curve-fitting) tends to produce a setting that performed great historically by coincidence, but has no real reason to keep working going forward.
Practice tip: Add both a 20-day EMA and a 50-day SMA to a chart you know well, and scroll back through several months of price action. Notice how differently the two lines react at the same turning points — that visual difference is worth more than any written explanation. Putting it together

Moving averages don't predict where price is going next — they describe, cleanly, where it's been and what direction it's currently leaning. The SMA and EMA give you two different lenses on the same underlying data: one smoother and slower, one faster and more reactive. Understanding that trade-off, rather than treating either one as inherently "better," is what lets you pick the right tool for the timeframe and strategy you're actually trading.
