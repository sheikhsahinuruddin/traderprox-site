Risk Management in Trading: Position Sizing and Stop-Loss Strategies

The math that decides whether you survive a losing streak — before you place a single trade.

7 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

Ask most beginners what determines success in trading, and they'll talk about finding the right entry signal. Ask a trader who has survived ten years in the market, and they'll talk about risk management first. This isn't false modesty — a mediocre strategy with strict risk control can survive and compound for years, while an excellent strategy with no risk control can be wiped out by a single bad week. Risk management is the part of trading that decides whether you're still in the game long enough for your edge to actually show up.

Why risk management matters more than entries

Every strategy, no matter how good, has losing trades. That's not a flaw to be engineered away — it's a mathematical certainty. What separates a trader who survives from one who doesn't is not the frequency of losses, but their size relative to account capital. A trader who risks 2% of their account per trade can lose ten times in a row and still have roughly 82% of their starting capital. A trader who risks 20% per trade can lose only a handful of times before the account is functionally destroyed, and needs an enormous, often unrealistic gain just to recover to breakeven.

That last point is worth sitting with: losses and the gains needed to recover from them are not symmetric. A 10% loss requires an 11% gain to recover. A 50% loss requires a 100% gain. A 90% loss requires a 900% gain. This mathematical asymmetry is precisely why avoiding large losses matters more than chasing large wins.

Position sizing: the core calculation

Position sizing answers one specific question: given how much you're willing to lose on this trade, how many shares or contracts should you actually buy? The calculation has three inputs.

  1. Account risk per trade — the maximum percentage of your total trading capital you're willing to lose on any single trade. A common range for individual traders is 0.5% to 2%.
  2. Entry price — where you plan to buy or sell.
  3. Stop-loss price — the price at which you'll exit if the trade goes against you, which defines your per-share or per-unit risk.

The formula is: position size = (account size × risk percentage) ÷ (entry price − stop-loss price). As an example, on a ₹5,00,000 account, risking 1% per trade means you're willing to lose ₹5,000 on this trade. If your entry is ₹200 and your stop-loss is ₹190, your per-share risk is ₹10. Dividing ₹5,000 by ₹10 gives a position size of 500 shares. Notice that the position size is entirely a function of where your stop-loss sits — a wider stop-loss means a smaller position, and a tighter stop-loss allows a larger one, but the maximum rupee amount at risk stays fixed at ₹5,000 either way.

This is the single most important habit to build: decide your stop-loss first, based on chart structure (a support level, a recent swing low, whatever your strategy defines), and let that decision determine your position size — never the other way around. Traders who pick a position size first and then figure out a stop-loss afterward are backing into their risk rather than controlling it.

Stop-losses: where and why

A stop-loss is a predetermined price at which you'll exit a losing trade, and it exists to answer one question in advance: at what point does this trade prove my original idea wrong? Placing a stop-loss based on that question — rather than on how much money you're comfortable losing — tends to produce better results, because the stop-loss is now tied to market structure rather than emotion.

A few practical guidelines for placement:

  • Beyond structure, not on it. If you're buying near a support level, place your stop a small buffer below that level, not exactly on it — support zones get tested and slightly pierced fairly often before holding.
  • Account for volatility. A stock that regularly moves 3% in a day needs more breathing room than one that moves 0.5%. A stop-loss set without regard to a stock's typical daily range gets triggered by ordinary noise, not by a genuine change in the setup.
  • Set it before you enter, not after. Deciding your stop-loss after you're already in a losing trade almost always leads to moving it further away "just to give it more room" — which is really just risk management breaking down under pressure.

Risk-to-reward ratio

Risk-to-reward ratio compares how much you stand to lose against how much you stand to gain on a given trade. If your stop-loss is ₹10 below your entry and your profit target is ₹20 above it, that's a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio — you're risking one rupee to potentially make two.

This matters because it interacts directly with your win rate. A strategy with a 1:2 risk-to-reward ratio only needs to win about 34% of its trades to break even, before accounting for costs. A strategy with a 1:1 ratio needs to win more than 50% of the time just to break even. Many profitable traders actually have a win rate under 50% — what makes them profitable is that their average winning trade is meaningfully larger than their average losing trade, which comes directly from disciplined risk-to-reward planning, not from being right more often than they're wrong.

Diversification and correlation

Position sizing controls risk on a single trade, but total portfolio risk also depends on how many trades you're running at once and how correlated they are. Being in five trades that each risk 1% sounds conservative, but if all five are long positions in the same sector — say, five different IT stocks — a single piece of sector-wide bad news can hit all five at once, and your real risk that day is closer to 5% than 1%. Thinking about correlation, not just the number of open positions, is part of genuine risk management.

The psychological side of risk management

Rules on paper only work if you follow them when it's uncomfortable to do so, and the moment a trade is losing money is exactly when discipline is hardest. Widening a stop-loss mid-trade because "it'll probably come back," or doubling a position size after a loss to "make it back faster," are two of the most common ways traders undo otherwise sound risk management. Both come from treating an individual trade's outcome as personally meaningful, rather than treating it as one data point among hundreds a strategy will produce over time.

Putting it together

Risk management isn't the exciting part of trading, and it won't make for a good story at dinner. But it's the part of the process that determines whether you're still trading — with capital intact — a year from now. Decide your maximum risk per trade, let your stop-loss placement determine your position size, aim for a favorable risk-to-reward ratio, and watch your correlated exposure across open positions. Everything else in trading, including a genuinely good strategy, depends on getting this part right first.
