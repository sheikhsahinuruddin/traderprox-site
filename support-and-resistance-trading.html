Support and Resistance: The Foundation of Technical Analysis

Why price bounces where it does, how a broken level flips from support to resistance, and how to use levels to plan entries, stops, and targets.

6 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

If you ask ten experienced traders what the single most useful concept in technical analysis is, a majority will say some version of "support and resistance." It's not flashy, it doesn't require a complicated formula, and yet it underpins almost every trading decision — where to enter, where to place a stop-loss, and where to take profit. Understanding it well is worth more than knowing twenty indicators superficially.

What support and resistance actually are

Support is a price level where buying pressure has historically been strong enough to stop a decline and push price back up. Resistance is the mirror image — a level where selling pressure has repeatedly stopped an advance and pushed price back down. Neither is a precise line so much as a zone, because the exact price where buyers or sellers step in shifts slightly each time.

The reason these levels exist at all comes down to memory and psychology. Traders and investors remember the prices at which they bought, sold, or wished they had done either. When price returns to a level where a large number of participants previously took action, many of them act again — the trader who regretted not buying at a certain price the first time is more likely to buy there the second time it's offered. That collective, repeated behavior is what creates a level that holds.

How to identify a real level

The simplest way to find support and resistance is to look for price points where the market has reversed direction more than once. A single touch tells you very little — it could be coincidence. Two touches is worth noting. Three or more touches at roughly the same price, especially across different time periods, is a level the market has clearly recognized as significant.

It helps to think in zones rather than exact prices. If a stock has bounced near ₹950 twice and near ₹947 once, that's a support zone roughly between ₹947 and ₹950, not a single laser-precise number. Traders who insist on exact-to-the-rupee levels often get frustrated when price reverses a few points early or late — the zone concept solves that frustration by building in the natural imprecision of real markets.

Round numbers deserve a special mention. Prices ending in 0 or 00 — ₹100, ₹500, ₹1000 — tend to act as psychological support and resistance even without a documented history of reversals there, simply because so many traders place orders around round numbers. It's worth watching how price behaves as it approaches them.

The role reversal: how support becomes resistance

One of the most useful and consistently reliable patterns in all of technical analysis is the way a broken support level tends to become resistance afterward, and a broken resistance level tends to become support. Once price breaks decisively through a support level, the traders who bought at that level are now sitting on a loss. Many of them are waiting for price to return to their entry point just to exit at breakeven — and that wave of selling as price approaches from below is what turns old support into new resistance.

The same logic runs in reverse for a broken resistance level. Traders who sold short at that resistance, expecting it to hold, are now losing money as price pushes through. When price pulls back toward that old resistance level, many of them buy to close their losing position, and that buying pressure is what turns old resistance into new support.

This role-reversal concept is genuinely one of the more dependable patterns you can learn, and it comes up constantly once you start watching for it — a stock breaks out above a resistance level, drifts back down to "retest" that same level, holds, and then continues higher.

Trendlines: support and resistance that move

Horizontal support and resistance assume a level stays fixed, but markets trend, and trending markets need a support or resistance line that moves with them. A rising trendline connects a series of higher lows and acts as dynamic support underneath an uptrend. A falling trendline connects a series of lower highs and acts as dynamic resistance above a downtrend.

The same "more touches equals more significant" logic applies here. A trendline drawn through two points is a guess. A trendline that price has respected three, four, or five times is a level serious traders are watching, and a break of that trendline — especially on high volume — is often the first visible sign that a trend is losing strength.

Using support and resistance in an actual trade

Support and resistance levels are most useful when they answer three practical questions: where do I enter, where do I place my stop-loss, and where do I take profit?

  • Entry: Buying near a well-established support level (in an uptrend) or selling near a well-established resistance level (in a downtrend) gives you a clearly defined reason for the trade, rather than entering at a random point in the middle of a move.
  • Stop-loss placement: A stop-loss just below support (for a long trade) or just above resistance (for a short trade) means you're only wrong, and out of the trade, if the level genuinely fails — rather than being stopped out by ordinary price noise.
  • Profit target: The next support or resistance level above or below your entry is a natural, objective place to take profit, since price often struggles to push straight through a level it hasn't tested before.

Common mistakes

  • Drawing too many lines. If every wiggle on the chart becomes a "level," the concept loses all meaning. Focus on the handful of levels with the most touches and the clearest historical significance.
  • Ignoring the timeframe. A support level on a 15-minute chart and a support level on a weekly chart are not equally important. Levels on higher timeframes generally carry more weight because more participants, over more time, have reacted to them.
  • Treating every level as unbreakable. Support and resistance are probabilities, not guarantees. Levels fail, especially when a major news event or earnings report shifts sentiment abruptly. Risk management — position sizing and stop-losses — exists precisely because no level is 100% reliable.
  • Confusing a level with a precise price. As covered above, thinking in zones rather than exact numbers avoids a lot of unnecessary frustration.
Practice tip: Pick five stocks you follow and mark the two or three most obvious support and resistance zones on each daily chart, based purely on how many times price has reversed there. Then watch how price behaves the next time it approaches one of those zones. Putting it together

Support and resistance won't predict the future with certainty, and no serious trader claims they do. What they provide is structure — a way to describe where a market has previously found buyers and sellers, and a framework for making entries, stop-losses, and targets less arbitrary. Nearly every other tool in technical analysis, from chart patterns to indicators, ultimately gets combined with support and resistance to build a complete trading decision.
