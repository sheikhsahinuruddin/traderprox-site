RSI Indicator Explained: How to Trade Overbought and Oversold Conditions

One of the most misused indicators in trading — used correctly, alongside price structure, it's a genuine edge.

7 min read·Updated Jul 27, 2026

The Relative Strength Index, almost universally shortened to RSI, is one of the most widely used indicators in trading — and also one of the most widely misused. Traders new to it tend to treat it as a simple "buy when low, sell when high" signal, which works occasionally by accident and fails badly the rest of the time. Understanding what RSI actually measures, and the specific conditions under which it's genuinely useful, turns it from a coin flip into a real tool.

What RSI measures

RSI measures the speed and magnitude of recent price changes, expressed as a single number between 0 and 100. It's calculated by comparing the average size of recent up-moves to the average size of recent down-moves over a lookback period, which is 14 periods by default on most platforms. A reading near 100 means recent price action has been almost entirely upward moves with very little pullback. A reading near 0 means the opposite — almost entirely downward moves. A reading near 50 means gains and losses have been roughly balanced over the lookback period.

The key word in the indicator's name is "relative." RSI doesn't measure price itself, or even the size of a move in absolute terms — it measures momentum relative to the recent past. That's precisely why it's called an oscillator: it oscillates within a bounded range rather than trending upward forever the way price itself can.

Overbought and oversold: what they actually mean

The standard convention is that an RSI reading above 70 is considered "overbought," and a reading below 30 is considered "oversold." These are the two numbers most traders learn first, and unfortunately, the two numbers most traders misuse first.

The common beginner mistake is treating "overbought" as synonymous with "about to fall" and "oversold" as synonymous with "about to rise." That's not what these terms mean. Overbought means momentum has been strongly upward recently — not that price is about to reverse. In a genuinely strong trend, RSI can stay above 70 for extended periods while price keeps climbing, and a trader who shorted the moment RSI crossed 70 would have been fighting a strong trend and losing money the entire time. The same applies in reverse for oversold readings during a strong downtrend.

RSI extremes are more reliably useful in range-bound, non-trending markets, where price oscillates between a ceiling and a floor rather than pushing persistently in one direction. In that kind of environment, an overbought reading near the top of the range and an oversold reading near the bottom genuinely do correspond to likely turning points, because there's no strong trend overpowering the signal.

Divergence: RSI's more reliable signal

Many experienced traders consider divergence to be a more dependable RSI signal than simple overbought/oversold readings, precisely because divergence works in trending markets, not just range-bound ones.

Bearish divergence occurs when price makes a new high, but RSI makes a lower high than it did on the previous price peak. This tells you that while price is still pushing upward, the momentum behind that push is actually weakening — fewer buyers are showing up relative to the last high, even though price technically got there. It's often an early warning that an uptrend is losing steam before that shows up clearly in price itself.

Bullish divergence is the mirror image: price makes a new low, but RSI makes a higher low than it did on the previous price trough. Selling pressure is technically pushing price to a new low, but with less underlying momentum than before — a potential early sign that the downtrend is running out of energy.

Divergence isn't an instant, precise entry signal on its own — it's a warning that the current trend's momentum is fading, which is valuable information to combine with support/resistance levels or candlestick patterns for an actual entry decision.

Using the RSI midline

Less commonly discussed than overbought/oversold levels, but genuinely useful: the 50 midline on RSI. In an uptrend, RSI tends to stay above 50 during pullbacks and only dips below it when the trend is in real trouble. In a downtrend, the reverse holds — RSI tends to stay below 50 and only pushes above it when the downtrend is genuinely losing control. Watching whether RSI holds above or below 50 during pullbacks can help distinguish a healthy trend continuation from a trend that's actually breaking down.

Adjusting the RSI period

The default 14-period setting is a reasonable general-purpose choice, but it isn't the only option. A shorter period (say, 7 or 9) makes RSI more sensitive and reactive, useful for short-term or intraday trading, at the cost of more false signals from ordinary noise. A longer period (say, 21 or 25) smooths RSI out further, better suited to swing or position trading where you care about the broader momentum picture rather than every short-term wiggle. As with moving averages, there's no universally correct setting — it should match the timeframe and holding period of the actual strategy you're running.

Combining RSI with other tools

RSI works best as one piece of evidence in a broader case, not as a standalone trigger. A few combinations traders commonly use:

  • RSI + support/resistance: An oversold RSI reading occurring exactly at a well-established support level is a stronger case for a bounce than an oversold reading in the middle of nowhere on the chart.
  • RSI + candlestick patterns: A bullish candlestick reversal pattern (like a hammer) forming while RSI shows bullish divergence gives you two independent signals agreeing with each other.
  • RSI + trend direction: Using RSI pullbacks toward 50 (rather than extreme readings toward 30) as entry points in the direction of an already-established trend, rather than trying to pick tops and bottoms against the trend.

Common mistakes

  • Fighting strong trends based on overbought/oversold alone. This is the single most common and most costly RSI mistake — shorting into strength or buying into weakness purely because RSI crossed 70 or 30.
  • Ignoring which type of market you're in. The same RSI reading means something different in a trending market versus a range-bound one, and treating them identically leads to poor decisions.
  • Using RSI in isolation. Like every indicator covered so far, RSI is far more reliable as a supporting signal alongside price structure than as a complete strategy on its own.
Practice tip: Pull up a chart in a clear, strong trend and note how long RSI can stay above 70 (or below 30) while the trend continues uninterrupted. Then compare that to a genuinely range-bound, choppy chart and see how differently the same RSI levels behave there. Putting it together

RSI is a genuinely useful tool once you stop treating "overbought" and "oversold" as automatic reversal signals and start reading them as descriptions of recent momentum instead. Divergence, the 50 midline, and context about whether the market is trending or range-bound all add nuance that the raw 70/30 rule misses entirely. Used carefully, alongside price structure rather than instead of it, RSI earns its place as one of the most popular indicators in trading.
